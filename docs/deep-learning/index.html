02 / The central idea

Learn the representation, too.

Composition creates expressiveness

A neural network applies a sequence of parameterized transformations. Early layers can build intermediate features that later layers combine. These representations are learned jointly for the training objective, instead of all being specified by hand. Depth refers to composing layers, not simply having many parameters.

Nonlinearity matters

Stacking only affine transformations still yields a single affine transformation. Activation functions such as ReLU or tanh allow the network to represent nonlinear relationships. Their derivatives also affect how gradients travel during training.

A family, not a learning regime

Neural networks can learn from labeled examples, targets constructed from the data, rewards, or combinations of objectives. “Deep learning” describes a model family; “supervised learning” describes the feedback available. These ideas overlap rather than compete.

Backpropagation is differentiation

Backpropagation efficiently computes derivatives through the computational graph. It does not itself choose how to update parameters. Gradient descent, momentum, and Adam are optimization procedures that use those derivatives. Keeping these roles separate helps make sense of the training loop.

Optimization is not generalization

A falling training loss means the optimizer is improving the training objective. It does not prove that the representation is useful on new data. Hold out evaluation data, monitor overfitting, and consider regularization, augmentation, or early stopping. Data quality and evaluation design still matter at scale.

06 / The mathematical machinery

Track the values. Then the derivatives.

The exact network in the playground
h = φ(g · (Wx + b))
W = [[1.2, −0.8], [−0.7, 1.3], [0.9, 0.6]]
b = [0.2, −0.1, −0.4]
z = 0.8h₁ − 1.1h₂ + 0.7h₃ − 0.2
p = σ(z)

The slider g scales the hidden weights and biases. φ is tanh or ReLU; the output always uses sigmoid. These fixed weights were chosen for illustration and have not been learned from data. A probability-shaped output alone does not imply calibrated predictions.

The chain rule makes learning possible
∂L/∂w = (∂L/∂a) · (∂a/∂z) · (∂z/∂w)
w ← w − η ∂L/∂w

For a unit with z = wx + b and a = φ(z), the local factor ∂z/∂w is x. The activation derivative gates how the downstream gradient reaches w. η is the learning rate; too large a step can destabilize training, while too small a step can slow progress.

Activation functions shape values and gradients
ReLU(z) = max(0,z)
tanh′(z) = 1 − tanh²(z)

ReLU has zero derivative for negative inputs and derivative one for positive inputs; implementations choose a convention at zero. A unit stuck on the negative side may stop receiving useful gradients. Tanh is smooth but saturates toward −1 or 1, where derivatives become small. Try increasing the hidden scale and observe saturation.

Why linear layers alone are not enough
W₂(W₁x + b₁) + b₂ = (W₂W₁)x + (W₂b₁ + b₂)

Composing affine layers collapses to one affine map. Nonlinear activations prevent this collapse and enable richer decision functions. Greater expressiveness still requires appropriate data, optimization, and evaluation to become useful.

Batch, epoch, and learning rate

A batch is the subset of examples used for an update. An epoch is one pass through the training set. The learning rate controls the update scale. Batch size affects gradient noise and computational behavior; it is not interchangeable with the number of epochs.

Attention in one equation
Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ / √dₖ)V

Queries and keys determine normalized weights over values. The scaling helps control dot-product magnitude. Positional information and masks constrain how tokens interact. Attention weights are part of the computation, not automatically a complete explanation of the model’s decision.
